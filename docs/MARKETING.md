# Vibes Marketing Foundation

- **Status:** working positioning and launch-material brief
- **Audience:** product, design, community, contributors, press, and future launch-site work

## Brand idea

**Vibes turns game development into part of the game.**

Players enter a beautiful shared world, experience it together, and propose what would make the next visit better. Local AI helps turn a feeling into a clear idea. The whole eligible group must approve the same proposal. A richer planner then creates a traceable, implementation-ready GitHub issue that developers can actually build.

The emotional promise is not “an AI makes infinite content.” It is:

> We played this together. We agreed on what would make it better. Now we can trace what happens to that idea—and, when contributors choose and ship it, see it become part of the world.

## Primary tagline

**Play the world. Propose its future. Build it together.**

Alternates for specific contexts:

- **The shared world that listens to the whole group.**
- **A multiplayer adventure with player-approved development proposals.**
- **Where playing together becomes building together.**
- **Your shared world. Your shared vote. A proposal everyone can trace.**

Avoid “the game that builds itself.” Vibes deliberately does not bypass design, engineering, review, or release work.

## Category

Lead with a familiar noun and make the new behavior the hook:

> **A self-hostable cooperative open-world adventure with a player-governed development loop.**

Useful secondary categories:

- open-development game, with the final open-source license still to be selected;
- cooperative world-building community, without implying freeform construction;
- multiplayer participatory design experiment;
- local-first AI game;
- playable development-proposal loop.

Do not lead with “DAO,” “metaverse,” “decentralized MMO,” or “AI-generated game.” Those terms imply economics, scale, consensus, or content generation that Vibes does not promise.

## Reusable pitches

### One sentence

Vibes is a self-hostable multiplayer open-world game where players turn feedback into proposals, approve them unanimously, and send the accepted ideas to GitHub as real development plans.

### 30-second pitch

Vibes begins as an original cooperative open-world adventure that friends can host on their own machines. While they play, anyone can suggest an improvement. A local model helps clarify the idea, everyone in the frozen eligible group votes on the exact proposal, and unanimous approval creates a repository-aware GitHub issue. The world stays fun first, people stay in control, and AI-assisted development becomes a visible multiplayer metagame instead of a hidden production tool.

### Long description

Most online games ask players for feedback far away from the moment that inspired it, then send that feedback into an invisible backlog. Vibes makes the loop part of the world. Small trusted groups explore a peer-hosted adventure, capture ideas in context, use local AI to make those ideas legible, and decide together which ones deserve development attention. Every accepted proposal has explicit consent, a verifiable history, and a traceable GitHub issue; every shipped change still passes ordinary design, code review, testing, and release controls. Over time, independently run Vibes worlds can develop histories and personalities shaped by the communities that actually play them.

## Positioning pillars

### 1. Fun before novelty

The first trailer beat is movement through a striking world, not a form or chatbot. Vibes earns attention through exploration, gliding, atmosphere, cooperative discovery, and a memorable shared finale. Governance is the unexpected second act.

**Evidence target:** Resonance Reach, responsive traversal, three distinct regions, cooperative Shard objective, Beacon transformation.

### 2. Players are co-authors, not prompt operators

AI helps people express an idea and plan approved work. It does not replace taste, consent, engineering, or community memory.

**Evidence target:** author review, immutable proposal, frozen electorate, unanimous thumbs, approved text preserved verbatim.

### 3. A world the community can actually own

Instances, saves, trust policy, and supporting services can be run on community-controlled machines. The design is honest that internet P2P still needs discovery and often a relay.

**Evidence target:** Vibes Node, portable world exports, replaceable rendezvous/TURN, self-host bundle, explicit privacy mode.

### 4. A development proposal you can trace

The loop does not end at a vague “AI is working” animation. Players can see proposal, consent, planning, issue, implementation, and release states.

**Evidence target:** signed certificate, one issue URL, build/context provenance, later webhook status in the Conclave.

### 5. Small groups with real trust

The first product is for friends and intentional communities, not anonymous mass governance. This makes unanimous consent meaningful and gives the game room to learn before public-world abuse problems dominate it.

**Evidence target:** invite-only worlds, persistent fingerprints, roles, visible authority, moderation/audit tools.

## Audience priorities

### Primary: player-builders

People who play co-op games, follow development, use mods, report bugs thoughtfully, contribute to open source, or already mix creative and technical hobbies.

Message: **Your taste can become visible, agreed-upon work—not a comment lost in a feedback channel.**

### Primary: friend groups and small communities

Groups looking for a persistent shared place with an identity of its own.

Message: **Host a world together, finish a memorable adventure, and decide what it should become next.**

### Secondary: contributors and maintainers

Developers, artists, writers, audio designers, QA, accessibility specialists, and technical designers who want player-grounded issues rather than speculative feature lists.

Message: **Every approved issue begins with a real play context, explicit scope, acceptance criteria, and a community waiting to try it.**

### Secondary: creative technologists and researchers

People exploring local AI, P2P software, online trust, participatory design, and human-in-the-loop development.

Message: **Vibes is being built as a real game and an inspectable experiment, with its tradeoffs documented rather than hidden behind “decentralized” claims.**

## Product vocabulary

Use names consistently but treat lore terms as working until art exploration validates them.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Vibes world** | one community-owned world identity, policy, history, and save |
| **Vibes instance** | a currently running session of that world |
| **Vibes Node** | player-owned authority/persistence process |
| **Wayfinder** | player character working name |
| **Resonance Reach** | first world/vertical slice working name |
| **The Loom** | central landmark and proposal gathering place |
| **Conclave** | shared proposal/vote interface |
| **Proposal** | author-confirmed immutable player outcome submitted to the group |
| **Approval certificate** | verifiable evidence that every eligible player approved |

Prefer plain words in onboarding. Lore should support comprehension, never make “proposal,” “vote,” “host,” or “privacy” ambiguous.

## 75-second reveal trailer

### 0–12 seconds — The world

- Sunrise over Resonance Reach.
- A Wayfinder sprints through Murmurwood, mantles a root, and looks up at Stormglass Aerie.
- On-screen: **A world worth exploring.**

### 12–27 seconds — The friends

- Invite link opens; three friends appear at the Loom.
- They glide into Whistling Cut, pass a Tuning Stone, pulse a Wisp, and solve the cooperative route.
- On-screen: **Hosted by you. Shared with friends.**

### 27–40 seconds — The payoff

- Final Shard enters the Loom.
- Music layers resolve; clouds separate; the Beacon ignites; the group glides toward the overlook.
- Let this spectacle breathe before explaining the twist.

### 40–52 seconds — The idea

- A player opens the Conclave at the overlook and types: “It was hard to see where everyone went while gliding.”
- Local refinement turns it into a concise “Shared glider trails” proposal with clear outcome and accessibility note.
- On-screen: **Turn a feeling into a proposal—locally.**

### 52–63 seconds — The agreement

- All four players see the same card and frozen roster.
- Four thumbs rise; the proposal becomes approved.
- On-screen: **Nothing moves forward until everyone agrees.**

### 63–72 seconds — The development plan

- Planning status resolves to a real GitHub issue with scope, acceptance criteria, and tests.
- The issue link appears as a new light in the Loom.
- On-screen: **From shared play to real development work.**

### 72–75 seconds — Title

- Vibes logo and primary tagline.
- End on world ambience, not a synthetic AI voice.

## Messaging guardrails

### Say

- “self-hostable” or “peer-hosted gameplay”;
- “small trusted worlds”;
- “local refinement” and the disclosed runtime boundary;
- “unanimously approved proposal”;
- “repository-aware plan”;
- “issue creation, not automatic implementation”;
- “original open-world adventure”;
- “replaceable signaling and relay infrastructure.”

### Do not say

- “serverless” — signaling and often TURN remain necessary;
- “fully decentralized” — the simulation has an authority and the MVP uses a lease service;
- “cheat-proof” — a malicious player-owned authority remains powerful;
- “every player in the world” without explaining the frozen eligible electorate;
- “private” without explaining peers, relay mode, model provider, target-repository visibility, and GitHub retention;
- “the game builds itself” or “autonomous development”;
- “infinite world” or “MMO”;
- comparisons that use another game’s protected names/assets as public identity;
- “democratic” as shorthand for unanimous trusted-group consent;
- “AI understood what players want” — the author confirms the exact text.

## Early FAQ

### Is the game really peer to peer?

Realtime gameplay establishes WebRTC sessions with a player-owned authority. A small signaling service helps peers find each other, and a TURN relay may carry some or all traffic when a direct path fails or privacy mode requires relay. Both can be self-hosted.

### Why is one peer authoritative?

Shared physics, inventory, puzzles, and persistence need one answer for an instant in time. A visible elected authority gives the world that answer; signed governance remains separately verifiable.

### Does AI change the running game?

No. A small local model helps write a proposal. After unanimous approval, a richer model helps write a GitHub plan. Humans still review, implement, test, merge, release, and choose when a world upgrades.

### What if one player votes no or leaves?

A no rejects that proposal revision. A disconnected eligible player remains required and may reconnect; missing votes expire rather than becoming approval. New joiners observe the current vote but do not change its roster.

### Who controls GitHub credentials?

A least-privilege publisher process controlled by the world operator or official Vibes service. Credentials never enter the game browser or an LLM prompt.

### Can communities run different versions?

That is the long-term direction. Each world pins a signed release and migration state. Updates are explicit and recoverable, not silently injected.

### Is this a clone of an existing open-world game?

No. Vibes learns from the genre’s standards for freedom, traversal, systems, and wonder while building original mechanics, places, characters, language, art, and audio.

## Community launch sequence

1. Publish the planning foundation and architecture tradeoffs.
2. Share short movement/world graybox clips before discussing AI; establish the fun-first expectation.
3. Recruit a small cross-disciplinary design council for art, accessibility, networking, trust, and self-host operations.
4. Open structured playtest notes around observable questions, not broad feature voting.
5. Run the first private two-to-eight-player worlds with mock proposal publication.
6. Enable real sandbox publication and publicly report duplicate, unauthorized, failure-recovery, and latency evidence.
7. Allowlist official GitHub publication for a controlled alpha and show the complete first proposal-to-release story.
8. Broaden self-host access only after onboarding, backups, relay/privacy, abuse, and recovery have evidence.

## Marketing success evidence

Avoid vanity metrics as the only signal. The first narrative is working when:

- most viewers describe Vibes first as a game or shared world, not “an AI demo”;
- first-time testers can explain the proposal/vote/issue boundary accurately;
- players finish Resonance Reach and voluntarily submit feedback grounded in what they experienced;
- approved proposals are specific enough for contributors and still recognizable to their authors;
- world operators can explain P2P, authority, relay, privacy, and recovery without surprise;
- the public can trace one idea from play session to issue, implementation, reviewed release, and changed world;
- contributor interest spans art/design/audio/accessibility/testing as well as code.

The strongest launch artifact will not be a feature list. It will be one honest story: a group played, noticed something together, agreed on a better experience, contributors shipped it, and the next session visibly carried that history.
